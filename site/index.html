Fuck deepfakes.
Prove your work.

Source Kit is a cryptographic camera app that signs each photo and video as you take it, on the device, without a network. The signature and the sensor readings go inside the file. Anyone can check them without needing anything from me. The manifest is standard C2PA, checked in CI against c2patool on every run.

I'm a journalist turned product designer, not a cryptographer or a career engineer. Everything is published — camera, cryptography, native modules, interface, test suite — because people who know more will get further with it than I will.

Where this comes from

I was a journalist at the New York Times and am now a product designer at Google. Source Kit is neither job, and neither organization endorses it. I built it nights and weekends, wanting a camera that could show its work.

Twenty-seven test suites, cross-checked against the C2PA reference implementation on every run. I have also missed things a cryptographer, or a career iOS engineer, would find in an afternoon. I would rather you found them.

Apple, Google, the C2PA working group and newsrooms with security teams are working on this with resources I do not have. A side project can publish everything and name its limits without asking permission.

A photograph has never been proof

It only used to be expensive to fake.

Soviet censors airbrushed the disgraced out of group portraits for fifty years before Photoshop shipped in 1990. A photograph was never self-evidence. It was friction: a darkroom, a skill, an afternoon, with picture desks, wire services and libel law making lying expensive.

Generative models did not make images forgeable. They made forgery free and fast. The industry replaced friction with provenance: sign the file where it is made, carry the record with it.

It shipped. The Pixel 10 signs every photo inside the imaging pipeline, claim keys in a Titan M2 chip, timestamp authority on the same die, certified at the highest assurance level the C2PA conformance programme defines. Qualcomm put a signer in the Snapdragon trusted execution environment. Apple's Reference Image, in an iOS 27 beta, captures sensor signatures and hardware identifiers with the frame.

Moving the signature into silicon solves what no application can: pixels that never leave the chip between sensor and signature leave nothing in between to attack.

What it doesn't solve

It leaves four holes. Everything below this is an attempt at one of them.

one

The standard underneath has real problems

C2PA has had its first independent security analysis, and it did not go well. Details below.

two

No signature reaches the scene

Point a hardware-signed camera at a good monitor and every guarantee holds: the sensor did see those photons.

three

Signing a claim doesn't make it true

Civilian GPS is unauthenticated and spoofers are commodity hardware. In-pipeline signing binds a forged fix exactly as faithfully as a real one.

four

The credential dies in transit

Most platforms strip metadata on upload, so the manifest disappears at exactly the moment an image starts to travel.

In August 2025 a researcher found the Nikon Z6 III would sign an AI-generated image run through Multiple Exposure mode. Hardware-rooted, cryptographically valid, nothing broken: the picture was handed to the signer, which signed it as designed. Nikon revoked every certificate it had issued; a year later the service is still suspended.

Take the holes in order.

One: the standard

Two: nothing reaches the scene

This hole survives every improvement in hardware, and widens as the others narrow. The better attestation gets, the more it becomes the only way in.

Three: a signature doesn't make a claim true

A signature binds the sensor readings into the file. It does not make them true. The same goes for the system clock and for any location derived from the network.

The failure is presentational, not cryptographic: a spoofable claim shown in the register of a proven one. A design problem, which is the one part of this I am actually trained for.

Four: the credential dies in transit

The industry answer is a soft binding: a perceptual fingerprint that survives re-encoding, plus a repository to look the manifest up in. Source Kit commits that fingerprint today. The lookup half isn't built, and it is the most valuable thing left undone here.

What follows

The seal and the label take holes one and three, two lenses and Inspect hole two, the hard one. Hole four is unfinished business.

None of it is detection. You can't out-classify a generator, but you can raise what a forger has to keep consistent: geometry, motion, shadows, time. Lay it out so a person can weigh it in about four seconds.

What happens when you press the shutter

Six things, before the file exists, all of them on the device and offline.

Tying the signature to the hardware

App Attest certifies that a genuine build of your app is running on a genuine device but gives apps no access to the attested key, so the signing key cannot be attested directly. What I do instead is a commitment, and it is the piece I would most like a cryptographer to check.

It sits on top of the platform, not against it

Being straight about where this is weaker

Everyone protects the key in hardware: the Secure Enclave here, Titan M2 on the Pixel, the TEE on Snapdragon. The difference is how much untrusted code touches the pixels before they reach the signer.

Source Kit signs in the app's own process; the Pixel signs inside the imaging pipeline, where Android userspace cannot reach. Hook this app on a jailbroken iPhone and the Enclave signs pixels of your choosing. That's a real gap, and it's the one the silicon closes properly. The key binding keeps the attack per-device: an attestation is welded to one signing key, so it cannot be pointed at a key someone else controls. On iPhones, and on the Android phones that will never get in-pipeline signing, this is roughly as far as an app can go.

The interesting work is in holes two and three. The full comparison is in the threat model.

A nutrition label and a tamper-evident seal

Two objects, two jobs. The seal is binary: intact, or not. The label is itemised and claims no verdict.

Verified, authentic and trusted name a conclusion somebody reached, and nothing here reaches conclusions. The interface names the operation: signature mathematically valid, media unchanged since signing, proven tamper.

A test fails the build if a verdict word turns up in a summary position. It sounds fussy. The pressure to round a qualified result up is constant, and much of it came from me.

The card carries its title and limits inside its frame, because it gets read as a cropped screenshot in a group chat.

Two lenses

A screen is a plane. A room is not. It is the one check that measures the scene itself rather than testing a claim against what the physics predict.

The verification side is lab-tested and green. The capture path moved to Apple's virtual dual-wide device graph and hasn't been confirmed in the field on iPhone 17 / iOS 26. It isn't finished.

Checks a person can do, with no model in the loop

Inspect opens any sealed file and shows the record. It uploads nothing, runs no classifier, returns no probability.

Shadows

The sun's true elevation and azimuth are computable from the signed time and place. A scene lit from the wrong side at the claimed hour is visible without analysis.

Horizon

The committed gyro attitude predicts where level sits in frame. Compare to what you can see.

Environment

Barometric altitude, heading and recorded conditions, laid beside what the file depicts.

Pose trace

The signed motion path drawn against the optical flow of the frames.

Parallax

Computed on-device from the committed second view, with its validity range stated.

When nothing ran

A check that didn't run says it didn't run. Insufficient data reports insufficient. Nothing is inferred from absence.

None of this is a panacea. Any single check falls to someone who knows it is there. What the stack restores is friction: forgery becomes a physical production, consistent across geometry, optics, motion, audio and time, committed before the forger knew which checks would run. Not proof. What photographs used to have.

The people who need this most are the people it can most endanger

A provenance record is a dossier: where you were, when, on which device, sometimes who you are. For a photographer somewhere hostile that file is a threat as much as a defence. Disclosure is a capture-time decision, not an export option.

Every signal is a live toggle in the viewfinder. Each field is committed under its own salt: reveal one later and it checks out against the original signature. Withholding does not break the seal.

Offline is the architecture, not a mode

Where the interesting work is

Honest assessments of the ideas I like most. All of it is open: take any of it, no permission needed.

ENF narrow

Mains hum leaves a timestampable frequency signature in audio. It needs a grid reference database, indoor mains-lit sound and a long enough take. What matters today is capturing the raw LPCM master, because every delivery codec filters exactly the band ENF lives in. Do that now and the analysis stays possible later.

PRNU needs a corpus

Sensor-noise fingerprinting is well established and close to useless without a reference corpus for the specific device. As a double-PRNU rephotography check it's far more interesting — a photograph of a photograph carries two sensors' fingerprints.

Single-image rephotography compelling

Chromatic aberration follows a radial polynomial with quadrant asymmetry from real lens decentering; screen-originated CA is uniform. JPEG 8×8 block structure from a displayed image survives into the recapturing sensor's RAW as periodic Fourier peaks. Both are physics, both need no second lens, both work on a stranger's file.

Inherit platform credentials when they ship

Ingest Reference Image and Pixel's pipeline signature as ingredients rather than competing with them. Sensor attestation plus contextual commitment beats either alone.

Android, properly an opportunity

Camera2 exposes what iOS doesn't: per-frame capture results, concurrent physical camera streams with published intrinsics and pose translation, and RAW that's an actual Bayer mosaic rather than a computational render. A serious Android build could commit more than this one does. The core is platform-neutral; what's missing is the Keystore and Play Integrity equivalent of modules/secure-enclave.

LiDAR on pro hardware underused

A depth map committed at capture answers the flat-plane question directly, with no feature matching at all.

Newsroom-side services off-device

Reverse image search and AI-watermark checks are useful and inherently require sending something somewhere. They belong in the newsroom, behind explicit consent, never on the capture device.

Published error rates the real bar

Every check above should ship with a measured false-positive rate against a named, open corpus — screens and prints at graded distances, lighting and device classes. Anything that can't state its error rate shouldn't show a number. This is the long pole, and it's mostly shoe leather.

Two other things worth doing

A standalone verifier CLI. src/c2pa/verifyAsset.ts has no platform dependencies. Someone should make npx check a file.

A TSA trust list. Timestamping has no mature public root store the way the web PKI does. That's a gap for everyone in this space.

Prove your work.

One last thing about the fairies. Everything here would have sealed them without complaint. Elsie Wright used a real camera and a real plate. The cut-outs were propped up with hatpins at a real distance, in a real garden, in real light. Two lenses would measure genuine depth, because there was genuine depth. Every rung on the ladder would be reached, and every one of them would be telling the truth.

That is the edge of what provenance can do. It can tell you a file is what it claims to be. It cannot tell you the world was as the picture says. That was always the reader's job, and the desk's.

It doesn't solve misinformation. I don't think anything does. It gives an honest photographer a way to show their work, and it makes the alternative more expensive.

Apple and Google will attest sensors better than any application can, and eventually this should not need to exist. Until then it is all here. Take whatever is useful, fork it under your own name, or tell me where I have it wrong. That is the most valuable thing anyone could do with it.
