Fuck deepfakes.
Prove your work.

Source Kit is a cryptographic camera app that embeds each photo and video with a signed record of how it was made — which device, which instant, what the sensors read, what a second lens saw — so anyone can check later where a file came from and what has happened to it since.

The record is signed and sealed into the file at the moment of capture, as a standard C2PA manifest. It works without a network, and it can be checked with any C2PA tool.

Chasing instruments of truth

A photograph has never been proof. It has only ever been expensive to fake.

In July 1917 two girls in Yorkshire photographed some fairies. The images were examined by Arthur Conan Doyle, who found them persuasive, and by Kodak, which declined to certify them but conceded it could not prove them fake. The fairies were cardboard, copied from a children's book and held up with hatpins. What is striking about the Cottingley affair is not that anyone was fooled but that the question was already understood to be a technical one, a matter for Kodak, rather than a question about two girls and a hatpin.

"A photograph passes for incontrovertible proof that a given thing happened," wrote Susan Sontag in 1973. "The picture may distort; but there is always a presumption that something exists, or did exist, which is like what's in the picture." She did not mean it as a compliment. The essay became the opening chapter of On Photography, and the book is a sustained argument that the presumption is unearned — that photographs are "as much an interpretation of the world as paintings and drawings are," and that seeming innocent of interpretation is exactly what makes them persuasive. Sontag was naming a credulity she wanted her readers to lose. It has instead been the working assumption of newspapers, courts and juries ever since.

What the medium had was never integrity. It was friction. Retouching is as old as the negative, and Soviet censors airbrushed the disgraced out of group portraits for fifty years before Photoshop shipped in 1990. A convincing lie took a darkroom, a skill and an afternoon, and picture desks, wire services and libel law made it expensive to attempt.

Generative models did not make images forgeable. They made forgery fast and essentially free, which is lighter fluid on an already smouldering sense of reality.

Lawmakers have written more bills about synthetic media than about any other use of AI

State legislatures introduced 635 AI bills in 2024 and more than 1,200 in 2025, the first year all fifty states filed at least one, and 2026 passed both totals before most sessions closed. Across that pile the most-legislated subject is synthetic media — deepfakes and disclosure — ahead of chatbots, hiring, policing and the rest.

What the laws require, and what they cost you for ignoring them, varies by jurisdiction. What does not vary is the technology they lean on. The EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency obligations became applicable on 2 August 2026; California's AI Transparency Act became operative the same day. Both rest on the same two mechanisms, and both mechanisms are necessary. Only one of them still works when someone is actively trying to defeat it.

Watermarking marks what a machine made, and it can be washed off

Google's SynthID and Anthropic's text watermarking, announced on 11 August 2026 and built on the SynthID-Text approach, embed a machine-readable signal in what a model produces. Both are worth doing. The signal survives ordinary handling — resizing, re-encoding, a screenshot — and does not survive an adversary. Passing an image back through a diffusion model reconstructs it faithfully with the mark gone, one watermark can be used to overwrite another, and there are off-the-shelf tools that do it in a command. Open-weight models emit unmarked output by default. And nothing marks a photograph that was never generated in the first place.

That last gap is where the liar's dividend collects. Once everyone knows video can be faked, real video can be dismissed as fake. In 2023 Tesla's lawyers told a California court they could neither admit nor deny the authenticity of recordings of Elon Musk describing Autopilot's safety, on the grounds that a man that famous is a likely deepfake target; Judge Evette Pennypacker called the position "deeply troubling" and ordered him deposed. Two January 6th defendants ran the same argument about the video placing them inside the Capitol, and lost. Marking what is synthetic does nothing for the person who has to prove that something is not.

Provenance signs what a camera saw, and the signature travels with the file

In February 2021, Adobe, Arm, the BBC, Intel, Microsoft and Truepic folded two existing efforts — Adobe's Content Authenticity Initiative and the Microsoft and BBC Project Origin — into the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity. The first specification followed in 2022.

It inverts the detection problem. Rather than examine a file for signs of forgery, put a tamper-evident seal on it at the source, so any later change reads as a change. A watermark says a machine was involved; a provenance manifest says which device, which moment, and what has happened since. The seal is a signature over the bytes, so it does not wash off — it breaks, visibly, which is the point.

Four assurance levels grade the signer, and only two of them are available

A signature is only worth the process that produced it, so C2PA's conformance program grades the signer and writes the grade into the signing certificate. The specification defines four levels. The program currently issues two.

LevelWhat it requiresWhat has reached it
1 Documented key generation and storage and a reviewed security architecture, verified by self-assertion. Keys may be protected in software. Desktop and cloud tools — the ordinary tier for an application that signs on a general-purpose computer.
2 Everything in Level 1, plus hardware-backed key storage and dynamic security evidence: a live hardware attestation the device presents when it enrols for its certificate, so the authority checks the silicon rather than taking the vendor's word. The Pixel 10 camera, the first shipping phone to reach it. For a mobile app, Level 2 is currently only achievable on Android.
3–4 Defined in the specification for stronger isolation and independent evaluation. The program has not published the requirements or begun issuing at these levels. Nothing yet.

Source Kit signs at the software tier and says so in every manifest. It holds no C2PA certificate at any level; it is not in the program.

From bytes to photons

Between the sensor and the signature there is a stretch of code. How long it is decides what the signature is worth.

A digital photograph begins as electrical charge on a grid of sensor wells and ends as a compressed file. Something has to turn one into the other: read the wells, interpolate colour across the filter mosaic, correct the lens, reduce noise, tone-map, encode. That chain runs for tens of milliseconds, and every stage of it is code that could in principle hand the next stage a different picture. So the question that decides what a provenance signature actually proves is not how strong the key is. Everyone keeps the key in hardware. The question is how much of that chain sits between the photons and the signing, and whether any of it can be replaced.

On the Pixel 10, almost none of it can. Google signs inside the imaging pipeline on the Tensor G5; the claim key is generated and held in Android StrongBox on the Titan M2 security chip; a timestamp authority runs on the device, so a capture made with the radio off still carries trusted time. The frame is never handed to general-purpose code between the sensor and the signature, which means there is no seam at which a different image could be substituted. Photograph something with a Pixel 10 and the file can support a claim almost nothing else can: that these are the photons that struck the sensor.

Qualcomm took the same idea to the other end of the Android market, putting a signer inside the Snapdragon trusted execution environment so the pipeline is isolated from the operating system running above it. Apple's Reference Image, visible in the iOS 27 beta, captures sensor signatures and hardware identifiers with the frame — but it sends them to Private Cloud Compute and returns an authenticated copy, and it is not built on C2PA.

Source Kit sits at the far end of that chain. It receives a finished image from the operating system and signs the bytes it was handed, in its own process, with a key in the Secure Enclave. Everything upstream of that hand-off is code it cannot attest to. This is the ceiling for a third-party app on iOS, not a design preference: Assurance Level 2 for a mobile app is currently reachable only on Android. What the app can do is commit far more around the frame — a second lens, the motion of the phone, independent time — so that a forger has to keep several signals consistent rather than one.

Cameras got there first, and California is about to make it universal

Phones are the late arrivals. Leica shipped the first camera with Content Credentials, the M11-P, in October 2023, with a signing certificate in the body. Sony added capture-time signing across the Alpha 1 II and Alpha 9 III, Canon launched its Authenticity Imaging System for newsrooms in May 2026, and Nikon added C2PA to the Z6III in firmware — then withdrew it within a week, when a researcher used the camera's multiple-exposure mode to make it sign a composite it had not photographed. Nikon suspended the service and invalidated every certificate it had issued. The credentials from that window no longer verify, which is the standard working exactly as intended, and a fair measure of how new all of this is.

From 1 January 2028, this stops being a feature. California's AB 853 requires every capture device sold in the state — the statute names "video and still photography cameras, mobile phones with built-in cameras or microphones, and voice recorders" — to offer a latent disclosure carrying the manufacturer, the device, and the time and date of capture, and to embed it by default. Provenance metadata goes from a differentiator on a flagship to a condition of selling a camera in the largest state in the country.

Four gaps remain

None of them is closed by moving the signature into the silicon. Everything below this is an attempt at one of them.

one

The standard underneath has real problems

C2PA has had its first independent security analysis, and it did not go well. Details below.

two

No signature reaches the scene

Point a hardware-signed camera at a good monitor and every guarantee holds: the sensor did see those photons.

three

Signing a claim doesn't make it true

Civilian GPS is unauthenticated and spoofers are commodity hardware. In-pipeline signing binds a forged fix exactly as faithfully as a real one.

four

The credential dies in transit

Most platforms strip metadata on upload, so the manifest disappears at exactly the moment an image starts to travel.

In August 2025 a researcher found the Nikon Z6 III would sign an AI-generated image run through Multiple Exposure mode. Hardware-rooted, cryptographically valid, nothing broken: the picture was handed to the signer, which signed it as designed. Nikon revoked every certificate it had issued; a year later the service is still suspended.

One: the standard

C2PA has had its first comprehensive independent security analysis, and it did not go well.

Golaszewski et al., Verifying Provenance of Digital Media: Why the C2PA Specifications Fall Short (UMBC, 2026). Generators and validators disagree on which timestamp to trust. Revocation is weak enough that conforming validators accept manifests signed with certificates already known to be compromised. The exclusion range permits alterations that no validator detects. Conforming validators return different answers on the same file. And the conformance program certifies products without technical review. Their conclusion: don't stake journalism or legal evidence on C2PA yet. eprint.iacr.org/2026/804

Two: nothing reaches the scene

This gap survives every improvement in hardware, and widens as the others narrow. The better attestation gets, the more it becomes the only way in.

Three: a signature doesn't make a claim true

A signature binds the sensor readings into the file. It does not make them true. The same goes for the system clock and for any location derived from the network.

The failure is presentational, not cryptographic: a spoofable claim shown in the register of a proven one. A design problem.

Four: the credential dies in transit

The industry answer is a soft binding: a perceptual fingerprint that survives re-encoding, plus a repository to look the manifest up in. Source Kit commits that fingerprint today. The lookup half isn't built, and it is the most valuable thing left undone here.

What follows

Everything in How it works is aimed at one of these. The second lens and the on-device checks take gap two, the hard one. Gap four is not addressed here.

None of it is detection. You can't out-classify a generator, but you can raise what a forger has to keep consistent: geometry, motion, shadows, time. Lay it out so a person can weigh it in about four seconds.

How it works

Each of these is optional and switchable live in the viewfinder. What is off is recorded as deliberately absent, which is a different fact from silently missing.

A second lensThe cheapest counter to photographing a screen.

Nearly every flagship ships a multi-camera array, and almost nothing uses the second one for provenance. Source Kit seals a simultaneous downsampled ultra-wide frame into the same file, as a C2PA ingredient with relationship componentOf. Open the photo in any C2PA reader and the second viewpoint is there.

An ultra-wide sees far more of the room than the frame you composed. A monitor bezel, the edge of a laptop, the glow off an OLED panel — all of it lands in the second view.

Past that, the geometry. Two lenses a known distance apart see a flat plane identically and a real scene with a disagreement no homography removes. The frame and the calibration are both committed, so anyone can measure that residual themselves. Beyond a few metres the answer is insufficient, never passed.

Status: the verification side is lab-tested. The capture path has not been confirmed in the field on iPhone 17.

modules/exhibit-camera

The motion of the phoneHard to fake consistently with the footage.

A window of gyroscope and attitude samples from around the shutter, signed into the record and drawn against the optical flow of the frames themselves.

Near detail should move the way the gyro says the phone moved. A recapture of a screen inherits the screen's horizon, not the phone's — and a fake that matches has to stay consistent across two independent signals.

src/provenance/poseTrace.ts

A raw audio masterDelivery codecs destroy what forensic work needs.

Alongside the compressed track, an uncompressed 16 kHz master converted from the same native buffers, with its hash signed into the record.

Mains hum at 50 or 60 Hz leaks into any indoor recording and drifts in a pattern unique to a grid region — effectively a timestamp you cannot forge without the grid's own history. Delivery codecs filter exactly that band out. Capturing the master costs a few megabytes and keeps the analysis possible for as long as the file exists.

modules/capture-kit

Hardware attestation, bound to the keyNot just a real phone — this key, on this phone.

Signing keys are generated inside the Secure Enclave and cannot be extracted. Apple's App Attest certifies the device and the app, but gives applications no access to the attested key, so there is no direct way to say "and this is the key I sign with."

The workaround is a commitment: set the App Attest clientDataHash to SHA256(challenge ‖ signingPublicKey). Apple's certificate then vouches for exactly that key. An attestation cannot be lifted off a real phone and pointed at someone else's key.

src/lib/appAttest.ts

Independent timeThe only claim that does not come from your phone.

An RFC 3161 countersignature, verified cryptographically on-device rather than read off the token. And an OpenTimestamps receipt, which lands the record's digest in a Bitcoin block and gives a lower bound nobody controls.

Offline, the capture signs without either and says the anchor is missing. Queued anchors record their delay rather than backdating.

timestamp.ts · ots.ts

A post-quantum signatureA photograph may need to prove itself in twenty years.

Evidence outlives its cryptography. A photograph taken today might matter in a court, an archive or a history in two decades, by which time the signature protecting it could be breakable.

So every record carries an ML-DSA-65 signature over the same commitment as the ECDSA one. A few kilobytes now, against a file that quietly stops being provable later.

src/lib/pq.ts

Field-by-field disclosureThe record is a dossier. You decide what travels.

A provenance record says where you were, when, on which device, sometimes who you are. For a photographer working somewhere hostile that file is a threat as much as a defence.

Every field is committed under its own salt into a signed Merkle tree, which gives three states a verifier can tell apart: disclosed, withheld — committed but absent, with no ciphertext to attack — and never-recorded, declared at capture and bound into the root, so a withheld field cannot later be passed off as one that never existed.

Reveal one field later and it still verifies against the original signature. Or destroy the seed, and the withheld fields become permanently underivable by anyone, including me.

src/disclosure

Checks a person can runNo model, no upload, no probability.

Inspect renders the signed claims against independent physical expectations and leaves the inference to you. The sun's elevation and azimuth are deterministic from the signed time and place, so the card shows which way shadows should fall. The committed gyro predicts where level sits in frame. The motion trace is drawn against the optical flow.

A detector returns a number nobody can audit, and it gets worse exactly as generators improve. This inverts that. A check that could not run says so.

src/components/forensic

A verdict that refuses to be a badgeFive questions, five answers, no checkmark.

A checkmark borrows the authority of a body that supposedly did the checking. No such body exists here. So the result is a ladder of five separate questions, with the unreached rungs stating why. Unsigned renders neutral grey, never red: the absence of a credential is not evidence of tampering.

Verified, authentic and trusted name a conclusion somebody reached, and nothing here reaches conclusions. A test fails the build if one of those words appears in a verdict position.

src/lib/trustLadder.ts

It works with the radio offSealing and verifying both, with no network at all.

No accounts, no analytics, no launch-time network calls, and no registry address bundled in the app. The optional calls that exist — a timestamp authority, a Bitcoin anchor, an attestation relay you host — are named individually in the docs.

Apple's Reference Image sends the raw image, sensor signatures and hardware identifiers to Private Cloud Compute and returns an authenticated copy. That is a reasonable trade for most people. It is not available to someone who cannot afford to be seen talking to a server.

Where the interesting work is

Honest assessments of the ideas I like most. All of it is open: take any of it, no permission needed.

ENF narrow

Mains hum leaves a timestampable frequency signature in audio. It needs a grid reference database, indoor mains-lit sound and a long enough take. What matters today is capturing the raw LPCM master, because every delivery codec filters exactly the band ENF lives in. Do that now and the analysis stays possible later.

PRNU needs a corpus

Sensor-noise fingerprinting is well established and close to useless without a reference corpus for the specific device. As a double-PRNU rephotography check it's far more interesting — a photograph of a photograph carries two sensors' fingerprints.

Single-image rephotography compelling

Chromatic aberration follows a radial polynomial with quadrant asymmetry from real lens decentering; screen-originated CA is uniform. JPEG 8×8 block structure from a displayed image survives into the recapturing sensor's RAW as periodic Fourier peaks. Both are physics, both need no second lens, both work on a stranger's file.

Inherit platform credentials when they ship

Ingest Reference Image and Pixel's pipeline signature as ingredients rather than competing with them. Sensor attestation plus contextual commitment beats either alone.

Android, properly an opportunity

Camera2 exposes what iOS doesn't: per-frame capture results, concurrent physical camera streams with published intrinsics and pose translation, and RAW that's an actual Bayer mosaic rather than a computational render. A serious Android build could commit more than this one does. The core is platform-neutral; what's missing is the Keystore and Play Integrity equivalent of modules/secure-enclave.

LiDAR on pro hardware underused

A depth map committed at capture answers the flat-plane question directly, with no feature matching at all.

Newsroom-side services off-device

Reverse image search and AI-watermark checks are useful and inherently require sending something somewhere. They belong in the newsroom, behind explicit consent, never on the capture device.

Published error rates the real bar

Every check above should ship with a measured false-positive rate against a named, open corpus — screens and prints at graded distances, lighting and device classes. Anything that can't state its error rate shouldn't show a number. This is the long pole, and it's mostly shoe leather.

Two other things worth doing

A standalone verifier CLI. src/c2pa/verifyAsset.ts has no platform dependencies. Someone should make npx check a file.

A TSA trust list. Timestamping has no mature public root store the way the web PKI does. That's a gap for everyone in this space.

Prove your work.

One last thing about the fairies. Everything here would have sealed them without complaint. Elsie Wright used a real camera and a real plate. The cut-outs were propped up with hatpins at a real distance, in a real garden, in real light. Two lenses would measure genuine depth, because there was genuine depth. Every rung on the ladder would be reached, and every one of them would be telling the truth.

That is the edge of what provenance can do. It can tell you a file is what it claims to be. It cannot tell you the world was as the picture says. That was always the reader's job, and the desk's.

It doesn't solve misinformation. It gives an honest photographer a way to show their work, and it makes the alternative more expensive.
