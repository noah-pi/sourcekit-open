Source Kit is a camera. Every photo and video it takes is cryptographically sealed at the shutter, on the device, with the capture context bound into the signature and Content Credentials written into the file itself.

It works offline. Nothing is uploaded. Any C2PA reader can open the result, and the whole thing — cryptography, native modules, interface — is published here.

Where we are

The provenance stack, and the holes in it

There was never a golden age of photographic truth. Retouching is as old as the negative, and the Soviet censors were airbrushing the disgraced out of group portraits for fifty years before Photoshop shipped in 1990. What photographs had was friction — a darkroom, a skill, an afternoon — plus picture desks and wire services and libel law that made lying expensive by other means.

Generative models didn't make images forgeable. They made forgery free, and fast. The friction is gone and the scaffolding is thinner, so the industry went looking for a replacement and settled, sensibly, on provenance: sign the file at the source and carry a manifest with it.

That's C2PA, and it is now real infrastructure. The Pixel 10 ships native Content Credentials on every JPEG from the camera app, with claim keys in the Titan M2 and signing inside the image pipeline — the first phone to do it, at C2PA Assurance Level 2. Apple is close behind: strings in an iOS 27 beta describe an "Apple Reference Image" mode that captures sensor signatures and hardware identifiers.

Both are genuine progress. Both also have limits worth naming plainly, because a provenance layer that oversells itself does more damage than none at all.

Apple's design sends the raw image, sensor signatures, capture window and hardware identifiers to Private Cloud Compute, and returns an authenticated copy. Whatever the privacy engineering around it, the architecture requires a network round trip and a relationship with Apple. A journalist working in a hostile environment, on a burner, offline, cannot use that.

And the standard underneath both has just had its first serious independent audit.

Golaszewski et al., Verifying Provenance of Digital Media: Why the C2PA Specifications Fall Short (2026) — the first comprehensive independent security analysis of C2PA, including a formal-methods treatment of its core protocols. Among the findings: generators and validators disagree on the trusted timestamp; revocation policy is inadequate enough that conforming validators accept manifests signed with known-compromised certificates; the exclusion range permits undetectable alterations; conforming validators produce inconsistent results; and the conformance program certifies products without technical review. Their conclusion is that C2PA should not yet be relied on for journalism or legal evidence. eprint.iacr.org/2026/804

Two ways to defeat a signed photo without breaking any cryptography

rephotography

Point a real camera at a fake scene

Display a generated image on a good monitor, photograph it with a genuine attested device, and every cryptographic guarantee holds perfectly. The sensor really did see those photons. Sensor attestation — Apple's, Google's, anyone's — is definitionally incapable of touching this, because it certifies that photons hit the sensor and says nothing about what they depicted.

Worse: closing injection attacks concentrates all attacker effort here. The better attestation gets, the more this becomes the only road in.

forgeable claims

Sign a true record of false facts

A signature binds the sensor readings into the file. It does not make the readings true. GPS is the clearest case: civilian GNSS is unauthenticated, spoofers are commodity hardware, and a phone will report a confidently wrong position with no idea anything is wrong. The same goes for the system clock, and for any network-derived location.

The failure isn't cryptographic. It's presentational — showing a spoofable claim in the same visual register as a proven one.

The seal

What Source Kit does at the shutter

Source Kit doesn't try to replace the platform layer. It assumes it — and does the work that layer can't, because it sits above the sensor rather than inside it.

It starts with hardware attestation

Signing keys are generated inside the Secure Enclave and cannot be extracted. Apple's App Attest then certifies that a genuine build is running on genuine hardware — but Apple gives apps no direct access to App Attest keys, so you can't simply attest your signing key.

The workaround is a commitment. Set the App Attest clientDataHash to SHA256(challenge ‖ signingPublicKey). Apple's nonce extension in the attestation certificate then vouches for exactly that key, not merely for some key on some genuine device. The binding rides inside every manifest, so anyone can re-check it offline, years later, with no server involved.

It's designed to sit on top of what's coming

Reference Image and Pixel's pipeline signing attest the sensor. Source Kit attests the app, the context and the custody. These compose: when a platform credential exists, it becomes an ingredient in the record rather than a competitor to it. The strongest future file carries both — the platform saying this sensor saw these photons, and the record saying here is everything else that was true at that instant, and here is what you should not believe about it.

Then it seals, twice

ECDSA P-256 over a canonical record, and an ML-DSA-65 signature over the same commitment so a future break of P-256 doesn't quietly invalidate an archive. A minor note today; archives are long.

The manifest itself is spec-conformant C2PA — CBOR claim, hard binding, COSE_Sign1 — written into the JPEG, PNG, MP4, MOV or M4A. Any C2PA reader opens it, including c2patool and the Content Credentials verifier, and CI cross-checks every build against the reference implementation.

The label

A nutrition label and a tamper-evident seal

Two objects, doing two different jobs. The seal is binary and cryptographic: intact, or not. The label is descriptive, itemised, and makes no claim to be a verdict. Most of the design work went into keeping them apart.

The vocabulary follows from that. Words like verified, authentic and trusted describe a conclusion somebody reached; nothing in this software reaches conclusions. So the interface says what happened instead — signature mathematically valid, media unchanged since signing, proven tamper — verbs attached to operations that actually ran. A test fails the build if a verdict word appears in a summary position, which sounds fussy until you notice how constant the pressure is to round a qualified result up to a confident one.

There are no badges either, and the reason is worth stating. A checkmark is an institutional gesture: it borrows the authority of some body that has supposedly done the checking — a notary, a ratings board, a verification team. No such body exists here. A badge would be borrowed furniture, and the borrowing would be the dishonest part, not the cryptography.

What replaces it is a ladder of five separate questions, each answered on its own terms, with an unreached rung saying why it wasn't reached. Unsigned renders neutral grey rather than red: the absence of a credential is not evidence of tampering, and colouring it like a failure would smuggle in a claim nobody checked.

The card carries its title and limits inside its own frame, because the most common way this gets read is as a cropped screenshot in a group chat.

Two lenses

The one check that speaks to the scene

Everything else in the record concerns the file. This concerns the world in front of it — and it's the only part of the design aimed squarely at rephotography.

A screen is a plane. A room is not. Two cameras separated by a known baseline see a plane identically up to a single projective transform, and see a real scene with residual disagreement that no homography can remove. That difference is measurable, and it's geometry rather than machine learning: there's no model to fool, no training distribution to drift, and no probability to launder into a score.

So a stereo capture takes the frame you asked for, plus a downsampled view from a second physical lens, plus the calibration data — intrinsics, extrinsics, distortion — and commits all of it into the signed record.

Committing the second view, rather than just the computed answer, is the whole point. If only the number were stored, you'd have to trust the device's arithmetic. With the frame committed, anyone can undistort both views, match features, fit a homography and measure the residual themselves. It's the difference between a claim and evidence.

And it degrades honestly. Disparity falls off with distance, so past a few metres the baseline can no longer separate a plane from a scene. At that point the answer is insufficient — never passed. A test that silently turns into a pass when it stops working is worse than no test at all.

Status: the verification side is lab-tested and green. The capture path moved to Apple's virtual dual-wide device graph and is pending on-device validation on iPhone 17 / iOS 26. Said plainly because it isn't finished.

Inspect

Checks a person can do, on the phone, with no model in the loop

The Inspect tab opens any sealed file and shows the record. It uploads nothing, runs no classifier, and returns no probability. What it does instead is put the committed data next to the physics, in a form a human can adjudicate in about four seconds.

This is a deliberate reversal of the usual arrangement. A detector takes an image and returns a number nobody can audit. These checks take a signed claim and render it against an independent expectation — where the sun was, where the horizon should sit, what the weather was doing, how the phone moved — and let the reader see whether the two agree.

The reader is doing the inference. That's the feature. It means there's no training distribution to drift, no ROC curve to hide, and no failure mode where the model is confidently wrong about something a person would catch instantly.

It also means the checks are honest about silence. Insufficient data reports insufficient. A check that didn't run says it didn't run. Nothing is inferred from absence.

Shadows and sun position

From the signed time and location, the sun's true elevation and azimuth are computable. The card shows where shadows should fall. A scene lit from the wrong side at the claimed hour is visible without any analysis.

Horizon and attitude

The committed gyro attitude predicts where level sits in frame. Compare to the horizon you can see. A recapture of a screen inherits the screen's horizon, not the phone's.

Environment

Barometric altitude, heading and the conditions recorded at capture, laid beside what the file depicts.

Pose trace

The signed motion trace drawn as a path, next to the optical flow of the frames. Handheld motion is unmistakable, and consistent fabrication across both is genuinely hard.

Multiple lenses

The parallax measurement, computed on-device from the committed second view, with its validity range stated.

None of this is a panacea, and I want to be careful not to imply otherwise. Any single check can be defeated by someone who knows it's there. What the stack does is restore friction — it raises forgery from a prompt to a coordinated physical production, consistent across geometry, optics, motion, audio and time, all of it committed before the forger knew which checks would be run. That's not proof. It's the thing photographs used to have.

Privacy

The people who need this most are the people it can most endanger

A provenance record is, by construction, a dossier: where you were, when, which device, sometimes who you are. For a photographer working somewhere hostile, that file is a threat as much as a defence. So disclosure is a first-class feature rather than an export option.

Decide before, not after

Every signal is a live toggle in the viewfinder, so the decision happens at capture, in context, rather than in a settings screen months earlier. What's off isn't quietly missing — the record marks it deliberately absent, which is a different and more useful fact.

Selective disclosure

Every field is committed at capture under its own salt, so a later reveal is verifiable against the original signature. You can hand someone a file that proves the media is unchanged and the time is anchored, while disclosing nothing about location or identity — and they can still check what you did disclose. Withholding doesn't break the seal.

De-identified copies

A share mode that re-signs with a fresh key and strips byline, organization, precise location, network claims, transcript and the device-key linkage that would otherwise connect two of your photographs to each other. The copy carries a deidentified marker, so a verifier reads the absence as a deliberate redaction rather than as evidence of stripping — and the original capture time is preserved, so every remaining claim stays literally true.

Identifiable content is tagged as such

When a record includes something that can identify a person — a byline, a fine-grained location, a transcript — the interface says so at the point of sharing, not in a policy document.

And it burns

Destroying the master seed for an item makes the withheld fields permanently underivable. Not hidden — gone. The record says that too.

Offline is the architecture, not a mode

Sealing and verifying both complete with the radio off. There are no accounts, no analytics and no launch-time network calls of any kind — the app ships with no registry address bundled at all. The optional calls that exist (a timestamp authority, a Bitcoin anchor, an attestation registry you choose and host) are enumerated by name, and an offline capture simply signs without them and says so on the record.

This is the sharpest architectural difference from Apple's announced design, which sends the raw image and sensor identifiers to Private Cloud Compute and returns an authenticated copy. That is a reasonable trade for most people. It is not available to someone who cannot afford to be seen talking to a server, and that person is precisely who this was built for.

What's next

Where I think the interesting work is

Honest assessments, including of the ideas I like most. Everything here is open — take any of it.

ENF promising, narrow

Mains hum leaks a timestampable frequency signature into recordings. Genuinely exciting and genuinely limited: it needs a grid reference database, indoor mains-lit audio, and a long enough take. The thing that matters now is capturing the raw LPCM master, because every delivery codec filters exactly the band ENF lives in. Do that today and the analysis stays possible later.

PRNU needs a corpus

Sensor noise fingerprinting is well-established and near-useless without a reference corpus for the specific device. As a single-image check it's weak. As a double-PRNU rephotography check — looking for a second sensor's fingerprint layered under the first — it's much more interesting, because a photograph of a photograph carries both.

Single-image rephotography compelling

Chromatic aberration follows a radial polynomial with quadrant asymmetry from real lens decentering; screen-originated CA is uniform and symmetric. JPEG 8×8 block structure from a displayed image survives into the recapturing sensor's RAW as periodic Fourier peaks. Both are physics, both need no second lens, and both work on a stranger's file.

Inherit the platform credentials when they ship

When Reference Image and Pixel's pipeline signing are broadly available, ingest them as ingredients rather than competing with them. Sensor attestation plus contextual commitment is strictly better than either alone.

Android, properly an opportunity

Camera2 exposes things iOS doesn't: per-frame capture results, concurrent physical camera streams with published intrinsics and pose translation, and RAW that's actually a Bayer mosaic rather than a computational render. A serious Android build could commit more, and better, than this one does. The core is platform-neutral; what's missing is the Keystore and Play Integrity equivalent of modules/secure-enclave.

LiDAR, on pro hardware underused

A depth map committed at capture answers the flat-plane question directly, without feature matching. It's the cleanest possible rephotography check on the devices that have the sensor.

Desk-side services off-device, by choice

Reverse image search and AI-watermark checks are useful and inherently require sending something somewhere. They belong to the newsroom, behind an explicit consent step, and never on the capture device by default.

Published error rates the real bar

Every check above should ship with a measured false-positive rate against a named, open corpus — screens and prints at graded distances, lighting and device classes. Anything that can't state its error rate shouldn't be showing a number. This is the long pole, and it's mostly shoe leather rather than code.

Prove your work.

I don't think provenance solves misinformation, and I'd distrust anyone who told you it did. What it does is narrower and more durable: it gives an honest photographer a way to show their work, and it makes the alternative expensive again.

The platform layer is arriving and it's good news. It will attest sensors better than any app can. But it will still be silent on what the sensor was pointed at, it will mostly want a network, and — on the evidence of the first independent audit — the standard beneath it needs work before anyone stakes a court case on it. Those gaps are where this sits.

So all of it is here: the C2PA engine, the crypto, the native modules, the interface, the test lab. Take the parts that are useful, fork the whole thing under your own name, or just read it and tell me where I'm wrong. I'd rather this became five better projects than stayed one.
