Fuck deepfakes.
Prove your work.

Source Kit is a cryptographic camera app that signs each photo and video as you take it. The signature and the capture context go inside the file, on the device, with no network involved — so anyone can check it later without needing anything from me. It's a standard C2PA manifest, and CI checks on every run that c2patool reads it.

I'm a journalist who became a product designer. I'm not a cryptographer, and I'm not a career engineer. This is a side project, published in full — camera, cryptography, native modules, interface, test suite — because people who know more than I do will get further with it than I will.

Where this comes from

I was a journalist at the New York Times and I'm now a product designer at Google. Source Kit isn't either of those jobs. It's a side project, built nights and weekends, because I wanted a camera that could show its work and couldn't find one. It isn't affiliated with or endorsed by either organization.

So the honest framing: I've read the specifications closely and tested this as carefully as I know how — 27 suites, cross-checked against the C2PA reference implementation on every run. I've also almost certainly missed things a cryptographer or a career iOS engineer would catch in an afternoon. If that's you, I would genuinely rather you found them than didn't.

The people working on this properly — Apple, Google, the C2PA working group, newsrooms with real security teams — have resources I don't. What a solo project can do is try things quickly, publish all of it, and be straight about where the limits are. That's what this is for.

A photograph has never been proof

It only used to be expensive to fake.

Retouching is as old as the negative. Soviet censors airbrushed the disgraced out of group portraits for fifty years before Photoshop shipped in 1990. What a photograph had was never self-evidence. It was friction — a darkroom, a skill, an afternoon — plus picture desks and wire services and libel law that made lying expensive some other way.

Generative models didn't make images forgeable. They made forgery free and fast. So the industry went looking for a replacement for friction and settled on provenance: sign the file where it's made, and carry the record with it.

This is real infrastructure now, and both implementations are good. The Pixel 10 signs inside the imaging pipeline with claim keys in the Titan M2, at C2PA Assurance Level 2. Apple's Reference Image, visible in an iOS 27 beta, captures sensor signatures and hardware identifiers alongside the frame.

Two things are still open. The first is architectural: Apple's design sends the raw image, the sensor signatures and hardware identifiers to Private Cloud Compute and returns an authenticated copy. Whatever the privacy engineering, it needs a network and a relationship with Apple. A reporter on a burner in a hostile place has neither.

The second is that the standard underneath has now had its first independent audit, and it did not go well.

Golaszewski et al., Verifying Provenance of Digital Media: Why the C2PA Specifications Fall Short (UMBC, 2026). Generators and validators disagree on which timestamp to trust. Revocation is weak enough that conforming validators accept manifests signed with certificates already known to be compromised. The exclusion range permits alterations that no validator detects. Conforming validators return different answers on the same file. And the conformance program certifies products without technical review. Their conclusion: don't stake journalism or legal evidence on C2PA yet. eprint.iacr.org/2026/804

Neither of those is the deepest problem, though. The deepest problem is what a signature is for.

There's a second gap, smaller but easier to trip over. A signature binds the sensor readings into the file. It doesn't make them true. Civilian GPS is unauthenticated and spoofers are commodity hardware; a phone will report a confidently wrong position and never know. Same for the system clock, and for any network-derived location.

That failure isn't cryptographic. It's presentational — showing a spoofable claim in the same visual register as a proven one. Which is a design problem, and solvable.

Tying the signature to the hardware

This is the piece I'd most like a cryptographer to look at — either to reuse it or to tell me it doesn't hold.

Apple's App Attest certifies that a genuine build of your app is running on a genuine device. But it gives apps no access to the attested key, so you can't simply attest your signing key with it. What I ended up doing is a commitment, and as far as I can tell it's sound — though I'd feel better if someone qualified confirmed that.

It sits on top of the platform, not against it

Being straight about where this is weaker

Everyone protects the key in hardware — the Secure Enclave here, Titan M2 on the Pixel, the TEE on Snapdragon. That isn't the difference. The difference is how much untrusted code touches the pixels before they reach the signer.

Source Kit signs in the app's own process. The Pixel signs inside the imaging pipeline, with the key in a security chip and a timestamp authority on the same silicon. Nothing in Android userspace can reach in between. On a jailbroken iPhone, someone can hook this app's process and hand the Enclave pixels of their choosing.

That's a real gap, and moving signing into the silicon is a genuine achievement — better than anything a third-party app on iOS can do. What the key binding still buys is that the attack stays per-device: an attestation is welded to one signing key, so it can't be lifted from a real device and pointed at a key someone else controls. For iPhones today, and for the very large number of Android phones that will never get in-pipeline signing, this is roughly as far as an app can go.

Which makes the interesting question what doesn't improve when that gap closes.

Two things. Rephotography — point any camera, hardware-signed or not, at a good monitor, and every guarantee holds, because the sensor really did see those photons. And spoofable claims — in-pipeline signing binds a forged GPS fix just as faithfully as a real one. The Nikon Z6 III made the point sharply: that implementation was hardware-rooted, and Multiple Exposure mode still let a researcher get the camera to sign an AI-generated image. Valid signature, abuse upstream of it.

So as the injection problem gets solved by people with fabs, the work worth doing moves to those two. The approach in both cases is the same, and it's the thing this whole app is arguing for: don't try to detect the forgery. Raise what a spoofer has to keep consistent — the geometry, the motion, the shadows, the time — and then lay it out so a person can weigh it in about four seconds. Any one check falls to someone who knows it's there. Together they turn a prompt into a physical production.

A nutrition label and a tamper-evident seal

Two objects, two jobs. The seal is binary: intact, or not. The label is itemised and makes no claim to be a verdict. Most of the design work went into keeping them apart.

The vocabulary follows from the same rule. Verified, authentic and trusted all describe a conclusion somebody reached, and nothing in this software reaches conclusions. So the interface names the operation instead: signature mathematically valid, media unchanged since signing, proven tamper. Verbs attached to things that actually ran.

A test fails the build if a verdict word turns up in a summary position. That kept the copy honest as the feature count grew, because the pressure to round a qualified result up to a confident one is constant, and a fair amount of it came from me.

The card carries its own title and limits inside its frame, because the way this actually gets read is as a cropped screenshot in a group chat.

Two lenses

A screen is a plane. A room is not. That difference is measurable — and this is the one check that measures the scene directly, rather than comparing a claim against what the physics say to expect.

Status, plainly: the verification side is lab-tested and green. The capture path moved to Apple's virtual dual-wide device graph and hasn't been confirmed in the field on iPhone 17 / iOS 26. It isn't finished.

Checks a person can do, with no model in the loop

Inspect opens any sealed file and shows the record. It uploads nothing, runs no classifier, and returns no probability. It puts the signed claim next to the physics and lets you adjudicate.

Shadows

The sun's true elevation and azimuth are computable from the signed time and place. A scene lit from the wrong side at the claimed hour is visible without analysis.

Horizon

The committed gyro attitude predicts where level sits in frame. Compare to what you can see.

Environment

Barometric altitude, heading and recorded conditions, laid beside what the file depicts.

Pose trace

The signed motion path drawn against the optical flow of the frames.

Parallax

Computed on-device from the committed second view, with its validity range stated.

When nothing ran

A check that didn't run says it didn't run. Insufficient data reports insufficient. Nothing is inferred from absence.

None of this is a panacea. Any single check falls to someone who knows it's there. What the stack restores is friction — it raises forgery from a prompt to a coordinated physical production that has to stay consistent across geometry, optics, motion, audio and time, all of it committed before the forger knew which checks would be run. That isn't proof. It's what photographs used to have.

The people who need this most are the people it can most endanger

A provenance record is a dossier: where you were, when, on which device, sometimes who you are. So disclosure is built in, not bolted on as an export option.

Every signal is a live toggle in the viewfinder, which puts the decision at capture, in context, rather than in a settings screen from months ago. And every field is committed under its own salt, so you can reveal one later and have it check out against the original signature. Withholding doesn't break the seal.

Offline is the architecture, not a mode

Where the interesting work is

Including honest assessments of the ideas I like most. All of it is open — take any of it, no permission needed.

ENF narrow

Mains hum leaves a timestampable frequency signature in audio. It needs a grid reference database, indoor mains-lit sound and a long enough take. What matters today is capturing the raw LPCM master, because every delivery codec filters exactly the band ENF lives in. Do that now and the analysis stays possible later.

PRNU needs a corpus

Sensor-noise fingerprinting is well established and close to useless without a reference corpus for the specific device. As a double-PRNU rephotography check it's far more interesting — a photograph of a photograph carries two sensors' fingerprints.

Single-image rephotography compelling

Chromatic aberration follows a radial polynomial with quadrant asymmetry from real lens decentering; screen-originated CA is uniform. JPEG 8×8 block structure from a displayed image survives into the recapturing sensor's RAW as periodic Fourier peaks. Both are physics, both need no second lens, both work on a stranger's file.

Inherit platform credentials when they ship

Ingest Reference Image and Pixel's pipeline signature as ingredients rather than competing with them. Sensor attestation plus contextual commitment beats either alone.

Android, properly an opportunity

Camera2 exposes what iOS doesn't: per-frame capture results, concurrent physical camera streams with published intrinsics and pose translation, and RAW that's an actual Bayer mosaic rather than a computational render. A serious Android build could commit more than this one does. The core is platform-neutral; what's missing is the Keystore and Play Integrity equivalent of modules/secure-enclave.

LiDAR on pro hardware underused

A depth map committed at capture answers the flat-plane question directly, with no feature matching at all.

Newsroom-side services off-device

Reverse image search and AI-watermark checks are useful and inherently require sending something somewhere. They belong in the newsroom, behind an explicit consent step, never on the capture device by default.

Published error rates the real bar

Every check above should ship with a measured false-positive rate against a named, open corpus — screens and prints at graded distances, lighting and device classes. Anything that can't state its error rate shouldn't show a number. This is the long pole, and it's mostly shoe leather.

Two other things worth doing

A standalone verifier CLI. src/c2pa/verifyAsset.ts has no platform dependencies. Someone should make npx check a file.

A TSA trust list. Timestamping has no mature public root store the way the web PKI does. That's a gap for everyone in this space.

Prove your work.

One last thing about the fairies. Everything here would have sealed them, cleanly. Elsie Wright used a real camera and a real plate. The cut-outs were propped up with hatpins at a real distance, in a real garden, in real light. Two lenses would measure genuine depth, because there was genuine depth. The shadows would fall where the sun put them. The motion trace would look exactly like a girl holding a quarter-plate camera. Every rung on the ladder would be reached, and every one of them would be telling the truth.

That isn't a hole in this design so much as the edge of what any of it can do. Provenance can tell you a file is what it claims to be and came from where it claims to have come from. It can't tell you the world was as the picture says. That was always the reader's job, and the desk's, and it still is.

Provenance doesn't solve misinformation. I don't think anything does. It does something narrower and, I hope, useful: it gives an honest photographer a way to show their work, and it makes the alternative more expensive than it currently is.

The platform layer is coming, and that's good news — Apple and Google will attest sensors better than any app can. It will still be silent on what the sensor was pointed at, it will mostly want a network, and the standard underneath has real problems the UMBC paper lays out. Those gaps are where something like this can be useful for a while. Eventually it shouldn't need to exist, and that would be the right outcome.

So it's all here. Take whatever's useful, fork the whole thing under your own name, or read it and tell me where I've got it wrong — I'd count that as the most valuable thing anyone could do with it. I'd much rather this turned into five better projects than stayed one.
